Beta decay, one vertex at a time

A neutron becomes a proton when one of its down quarks changes flavor. The W boson carries off the charge that change leaves unbalanced, then turns into an electron and an antineutrino. Four instruments below let you step through the process, see why the W is so heavy to produce, watch the energy get shared, and follow the same step down a real chain of decays.

01

The vertex

Time runs left to right. Each step freezes the picture at the dashed line and tallies what exists at that moment. Three totals must come out the same at every step: charge, baryon number and lepton number.

On a phone, swipe the diagram sideways to see all of it.

An arrow pointing against the flow of time marks an antiparticle, which is why the antineutrino's arrow points back toward the vertex. The diagram is a calculational device and not a film of what happens.

Baryon number counts quarks, 1/3 each. Lepton number counts leptons minus antileptons.

02

Why the W stays virtual

A real W boson has a mass of about 80,000 MeV. Neutron decay has roughly 0.78 MeV to spend. So the W is never produced as a free particle; it is an internal line of the diagram. Its enormous mass is also a big reason the decay is slow. Drag the sliders to see both effects.

03

Where the energy goes

If the neutron decayed into only a proton and an electron, energy and momentum conservation would pin the electron to a single energy. Measured electrons come out with a smooth spread of energies. The antineutrino takes whatever the electron leaves behind.

04

The cascade

Real nuclei repeat the same step until they reach a stable neighbor. Each β− decay turns one neutron into a proton, so the nucleus moves one square up and one square left on the chart of nuclides while the mass number A stays fixed. Pick a chain and decay it step by step.

Horizontal axis: neutrons N. Vertical axis: protons Z. Each arrow is one β− decay (N falls by 1, Z rises by 1).

Rolling downhill

Each drop is the Q-value of one decay, the mass-energy the nucleus gives up. It leaves as kinetic energy of the electron and antineutrino, plus gamma rays when the daughter is left excited. The drops add up to the full mass difference between the first nuclide and the stable end.

What stays balanced

What is simplified

  • The electron spectrum uses phase space only (no Coulomb correction) and ignores the proton recoil, which stays under 1 keV.
  • The strength curve uses a spacelike momentum transfer, where the W propagator is mW2 / (mW2 + q2) relative to its low-energy value.
  • The "range of the force" is the heuristic ħc / (mc²), not a measured distance.
  • Half-lives are rounded. Q-values are ground-state to ground-state totals.
